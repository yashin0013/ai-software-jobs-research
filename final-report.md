# AI and Software Engineering Jobs: What Is Actually Happening?

### An evidence-based analysis of layoffs, AI adoption, developer productivity, hiring, and the changing role of software engineers

**Research period:** 2022–September 2026

---

## Executive Summary

The software engineering job market is changing, but the available evidence does not support a simple story that "AI caused the tech layoffs."

The large technology layoff waves beginning in 2022 occurred alongside the reversal of pandemic-era over-hiring, higher interest rates, reduced growth expectations, tighter venture funding, and aggressive cost reduction.

AI is now becoming an additional force.

It is changing how software is written, tested, reviewed and maintained. It is also changing what companies expect from engineers. However, the evidence available today does not establish that AI has already caused a broad collapse in software engineering employment.

The more visible change is **inside the job**:

> AI can reduce the amount of human effort required for some software tasks while increasing the importance of architecture, verification, domain knowledge, system understanding and judgment.

At the same time, there are signs that the entry-level end of the market is under greater pressure than experienced engineering roles.

This creates a more complicated picture than either:

> "AI will replace developers."

or:

> "AI changes nothing."

The evidence points somewhere in between.

---

# 1. What Happened to Tech Jobs After 2021?

The technology industry experienced extraordinary hiring growth during the pandemic.

When demand normalized, companies began correcting their workforce levels.

Crunchbase's U.S. tech layoff tracker records more than:

* **93,000 layoffs in 2022**
* **191,000+ in 2023**
* **95,667 in 2024**
* **~127,000 in 2025**

These numbers represent technology-sector layoffs rather than software engineers specifically.

That distinction matters.

A technology company announcing 10,000 layoffs does not mean 10,000 software engineers lost their jobs.

Layoffs can include:

* Recruiting
* Human resources
* Sales
* Marketing
* Operations
* Customer support
* Management
* Product
* Engineering
* Other corporate functions

Therefore, technology-sector layoff statistics should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of software-engineer replacement by AI.

---

# 2. Was AI Responsible for the Layoffs?

There is evidence that AI is influencing workforce decisions.

Some executives have explicitly discussed using AI to increase efficiency or accomplish more work with smaller teams.

But this is different from proving that AI caused the large layoff waves of 2022–2023.

The timing matters.

The first major waves began before today's current generation of AI coding agents became widely capable.

The broader explanation includes:

* Pandemic-era over-hiring
* Rising interest rates
* Declining technology valuations
* Slower consumer and enterprise demand
* Venture-capital contraction
* Pressure to improve profitability
* Organizational restructuring

Therefore, the strongest evidence-based description is:

> **The post-2021 technology layoffs were primarily a broad labor and business-cycle correction, while AI has increasingly become an additional factor affecting workforce planning and job design.**

This distinction is important because both things can be true simultaneously.

AI can change future workforce requirements without being the primary cause of the original layoff cycle.

---

# 3. What Does the Software Employment Data Say?

The overall software occupation has not disappeared.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics currently projects employment for software developers, quality assurance analysts and testers to grow **10% from 2025 to 2035**, with about **106,100 openings per year** on average. For software developers specifically, the projection is also 10%, from approximately 1.72 million jobs in 2025 to 1.89 million in 2035.

This does not prove that every software engineer will benefit.

It also does not tell us what happens to particular companies, countries, experience levels or specialties.

But it does provide an important counterpoint to the idea that software engineering as an occupation is already disappearing.

The labor market can simultaneously experience:

**fewer jobs at some companies + more software jobs elsewhere.**

Software is increasingly embedded in industries outside traditional technology companies:

* Finance
* Healthcare
* Manufacturing
* Automotive
* Logistics
* Defense
* Retail
* Professional services

Therefore, Silicon Valley layoffs and the overall software occupation are not the same measurement.

---

# 4. AI Productivity Is Real — But Highly Context-Dependent

One of the strongest arguments for AI replacing some engineering work is productivity.

If one engineer can accomplish the work previously requiring several engineers, companies may eventually need fewer engineers.

There is evidence that AI can produce substantial productivity improvements on certain tasks.

A well-known GitHub Copilot experiment reported significant speed improvements on a controlled programming task.

But other research shows a very different result.

METR conducted a randomized controlled trial involving experienced open-source developers working on their own repositories. In that study, developers using early-2025 AI tools took **19% longer** to complete the assigned issues. Developers themselves had expected AI to make them substantially faster.

This does not mean AI makes developers slower in general.

METR explicitly cautions against generalizing the result to all software development.

Instead, it demonstrates an important principle:

> **AI productivity depends heavily on the task, codebase, developer, tooling and environment.**

AI can be extremely effective at:

* Boilerplate
* Simple CRUD code
* Test generation
* Documentation
* Code transformation
* Small isolated functions
* Familiar frameworks
* Prototyping

The situation becomes more complicated when the task requires:

* Understanding a large existing codebase
* Debugging subtle production failures
* Understanding undocumented business rules
* Architectural decisions
* Security
* Distributed systems
* Long-term maintenance
* Coordinating changes across many systems

So measuring "lines of code generated" is not equivalent to measuring engineering productivity.

---

# 5. The Maintainability Problem

Another important finding comes from GitClear's 2026 research.

Its analysis covers **623 million code changes from 2023–2026** and tracks multiple indicators of code quality and maintainability.

Among its reported findings:

* Code-block duplication increased **81%**
* Error-masking constructs increased **47%**
* Refactoring activity fell substantially
* Moved/refactored code declined to **3.8%** of changed lines in 2026
* Cross-file reuse signals declined

GitClear describes this as a growing **maintainability gap**.

This creates an important distinction:

### Code production

AI can make producing code faster.

### Software engineering

Software engineering also includes:

* Reading
* Reviewing
* Testing
* Debugging
* Designing
* Refactoring
* Securing
* Operating
* Maintaining
* Understanding business requirements

If AI increases code production faster than organizations can review and maintain that code, some of the productivity gain can be transferred into future maintenance costs.

This creates a new engineering question:

> **Are we becoming faster at producing software, or simply faster at producing software that someone must maintain later?**

The answer is likely to vary significantly by organization and workflow.

---

# 6. The Biggest Change May Be Seniority, Not Total Employment

One of the most important patterns emerging from the research is the possibility of an increasingly asymmetric labor market.

AI is particularly effective at some tasks that historically provided useful work for junior developers:

* Basic UI implementation
* Simple API endpoints
* Boilerplate
* Test generation
* Documentation
* Small bug fixes
* Data transformations

That creates a difficult problem.

Traditionally:

**Junior developer → learns through simple tasks → becomes experienced engineer**

But if AI performs more of those simple tasks, companies may have fewer opportunities or incentives to hire beginners for them.

At the same time, organizations still need people who can:

* Understand the entire system
* Make architectural decisions
* Validate AI-generated code
* Debug complex failures
* Understand customers
* Manage security
* Make trade-offs
* Integrate multiple systems

This can produce a labor-market structure where:

> **The bottom of the career ladder becomes harder to enter while experienced engineers remain important.**

This is one of the most significant questions for the software industry over the next several years.

However, the current evidence does not prove that junior software engineers will permanently disappear.

It demonstrates pressure on entry-level opportunities, not the elimination of the entry-level profession.

---

# 7. AI Is Changing What "Good Developer" Means

The traditional model of software development placed enormous value on the ability to manually write code.

That skill remains useful.

But its relative economic value is changing.

An engineer who can produce 500 lines of code manually is no longer automatically more valuable than someone who can direct an AI system to produce and verify 5,000 lines.

The important skills increasingly include:

### System understanding

Can you understand how the entire system works?

### Specification

Can you clearly define what needs to be built?

### Verification

Can you determine whether AI-generated code is actually correct?

### Architecture

Can you decide how components should interact?

### Debugging

Can you find the real cause when generated code fails?

### Security

Can you recognize vulnerabilities introduced by generated code?

### Data engineering

Can you structure and move reliable data between systems?

### Domain knowledge

Can you understand the actual business problem?

### AI orchestration

Can you use models, tools, APIs and agents effectively?

The engineer increasingly becomes less of a pure code producer and more of a **system builder and verifier**.

---

# 8. What Happens to SaaS?

AI lowers the cost of creating software.

That creates both opportunity and competition.

A small team can now build:

* Prototypes
* Internal tools
* AI assistants
* Automations
* Dashboards
* Integrations
* Vertical SaaS products

much faster than before.

But cheaper software development also means more people can enter the market.

Therefore:

> **Code becomes less scarce. Distribution, domain expertise, proprietary data and workflow integration become more important.**

Generic software may become increasingly commoditized.

A product with:

> "We have better CRUD code"

has little defensibility when thousands of teams can generate similar code.

A product with:

> "We deeply understand this industry's workflow, own the operational data, integrate with existing processes and solve an expensive problem"

has a different kind of advantage.

AI therefore may not simply destroy SaaS.

It may change where the value of SaaS resides.

---

# 9. What Happens to Freelancers?

Freelancing is likely to experience the same split.

Low-complexity work becomes easier to automate:

* Basic websites
* Simple CRUD applications
* Basic landing pages
* Boilerplate APIs
* Simple integrations

This increases competition.

At the same time, new demand is emerging around:

* AI integration
* AI automation
* Private AI systems
* RAG
* Agent workflows
* Data integration
* AI-enabled internal tools
* Legacy-system modernization

The distinction is therefore increasingly:

**"Can you write code?"**

versus:

**"Can you solve a business problem using software and AI?"**

The second question becomes more important as the cost of producing code falls.

---

# 10. What We Can Say With Confidence

Based on the evidence reviewed, several conclusions are relatively well supported.

### 1. AI is changing software engineering.

This is already happening.

### 2. AI can significantly accelerate some development tasks.

But productivity gains vary by task and environment.

### 3. AI has not yet demonstrated that it has eliminated software engineering as an occupation.

Current U.S. BLS projections still show positive employment growth for software developers.

### 4. The major 2022–2023 tech layoffs cannot reasonably be attributed to AI alone.

The timing and broader economic evidence point to a combination of macroeconomic and industry factors.

### 5. AI is beginning to influence workforce composition.

The strongest pressure may appear first in routine tasks and some entry-level work.

### 6. More generated code creates a verification and maintenance problem.

The GitClear findings provide evidence that code-quality and maintainability metrics deserve attention.

### 7. Software engineering is becoming more than code generation.

Architecture, verification, security, systems thinking and domain knowledge remain important.

---

# 11. What We Still Don't Know

There are several questions that cannot yet be answered confidently.

### Will AI eventually reduce total software-engineering employment?

Unknown.

Higher productivity could reduce labor demand.

But cheaper software could also increase the amount of software businesses want to build.

Both forces can operate simultaneously.

### Will junior engineering jobs recover?

Unknown.

Companies still need experienced engineers in the future, which creates a potential incentive to rebuild junior pipelines.

But AI may permanently change what junior engineers do.

### How much engineering work will autonomous agents perform?

Unknown.

Current systems are improving rapidly, but reliable long-horizon software development remains an active research problem.

### Will AI productivity translate into fewer employees?

Not automatically.

Companies can use productivity gains to:

* Reduce headcount
* Build more products
* Enter new markets
* Increase output
* Reduce development time
* Increase profit
* Reallocate workers

Productivity and employment are related, but they are not identical.

---

# 12. The Central Finding

After comparing the two AI-generated research reports and cross-checking their strongest claims, the most defensible conclusion is:

> **AI is not simply replacing software engineers. It is changing the economics and composition of software engineering.**

The first phase of AI adoption has largely been about making individual tasks faster.

The next phase is likely to be about changing the structure of engineering teams.

That could mean:

**fewer people writing routine code**

while simultaneously requiring:

**more emphasis on architecture, verification, security, domain knowledge, data and system ownership.**

Whether this eventually produces a net decline or increase in software employment remains an open question.

The evidence as of September 2026 is not strong enough to settle that question.

But one thing is already clear:

> **The valuable skill is moving from simply producing code toward understanding what should be built, directing AI to build it, and being able to prove that what was built actually works.**
